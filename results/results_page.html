Numerical audit and autoresearch loop · “The Exponential Time Hypothesis is False” (OpenAI, Oct 2026)

When does the ETH-refuting algorithm beat brute force?

I modelled the paper’s counting algorithm (Sections 2–4, bounded-occurrence 3-CNF, its most favourable case), recomputed exactly where its own parameter checks pass and where its cost drops below known algorithms, then re-tuned it within its own constraints. The core construction was also implemented and tested in code. A follow-up research loop then cut the break-even against the best yes/no algorithm for this class (1.1199ⁿ) to log₂ n ≈ 196,700, and against 1.307ⁿ, the record for all 3-SAT formulas, from 2.22 million to about 10,100, using extensions whose proofs are only sketched; that section comes first. The paper’s second algorithm (Appendix D) with new steps counts exactly and would beat the best counting method for this class (2n/3) from about 525 and 1.1199ⁿ from about 2,000; its code passed the loop’s referee on small formulas, and its cost bounds are a model with proof sketches. Everything here assumes the paper’s theorem, which independent reviewers have not yet confirmed.

Round 7 (1 November, 20:10 to 21:05) followed three more outside reviews. GPT-6 Astra proposed doing each step of the group-by-group mode sum as matrix multiplication. Sorting the three index sets of a step by exact weight turns the step into one block product per allowed weight triple, the same sum term by term, and Strassen’s algorithm multiplies the blocks with 7 products instead of 8 per halving. In the cost model the mode fraction of the largest step drops from 0.612 to 0.570 at seven groups. I proved the reorganisation and the cost formula, checked them on 108 toy nodes in three mode bases (5,435 block products, every value equal to the direct one), and gave the referee hidden checks: Strassen’s product counts, the shape of every block multiplication against its own reference, node values and exact end-to-end counts; a schoolbook multiplication and a sum that bypasses the multiplication both fail them. T20: Qwen added the matrix-product version in one attempt of eight minutes; every hidden check passed, and the break-even against 2n/3 fell from 1,370 to 1,280, exactly as the what-if run had predicted (5,518 to 5,137 against 1.1199ⁿ, 1,044 to 977 against 1.307ⁿ, 368 to 347 against 1.637ⁿ). With the best known matrix multiplication exponent (about 2.37) instead of Strassen’s 2.81 the model would give about 1,180, but those algorithms are far too large to implement. The other proposals did not survive checking: Fable’s cap on the exponents per packet half changes the count by less than 0.01%, and Gemini’s ideas were either already in the model or cannot work, because the total weight of a block’s modes is fixed by the block itself.

Round 8 (1 November, 21:40 to 22:55) began with a question to GPT-6 Astra about Fable’s open problem. Astra kept the Cauchy blocks but changed the polynomial around them. Each packet gets empty guard slots at both ends (about a thousandth of its slots at each end at the chosen setting), and every entry of the deformation becomes a Lagrange interpolation polynomial of degree Q − 1 instead of 2Q − 1. At the interpolation points the matrix is still a pure shift, so the nodes stay local; near a node every crossing block is the old Cauchy matrix times a factor per row and a unit per column, so the LDU modes keep their weights; and because the polynomial has half the degree, the Hermite recovery needs half the 2-adic precision. I proved the steps, reran Astra’s own checks, and checked the construction in my exact toy: 75 nodes by four summation orders, 212 child networks and 41 exact end-to-end counts, which used 26 to 49% less precision than the paper’s deformation on the same formulas. The referee gained hidden checks for the new deformation; a wrong row factor and missing column units both fail them. Qwen wrote the code in one attempt; its own tests all passed after a quarter of an hour (I stopped it after 42 minutes, while it was still adding extra checks of its own), and every hidden check of the referee passed. The break-even against 2n/3 fell from 1,280 to 686, exactly as the what-if run had predicted (5,137 to 2,600 against 1.1199ⁿ, 977 to 531 against 1.307ⁿ, 347 to 204 against 1.637ⁿ and 187 to 126 against 2ⁿ).

Round 9 (2 November, 00:00 to 00:45) checked two more proposals from GPT-6 Astra: a Chinese-remainder reset that recovers each child’s high input precision from many low-precision calls, and a label balance for the children that charges the number of parallel wires between two vertices instead of the maximum degree. Both hold (I checked the arguments and reran Astra’s programs), but in the cost model neither lowers a break-even: every constraint that binds now sits at the root, where the arithmetic needs about 108 retained 2-adic levels with seven groups and the root’s own label balance and padding then fix the size. The children have room to spare, and that room is what the two steps enlarge. The search behind this check found something else: Qwen’s parameter search had missed the best setting of the current model, because it tried only two chunk sizes and pre-ranked candidates by the padding budget alone, ignoring the root’s label balance. Round 10 (2 November, 11:36 to 13:46) built a proposal of GPT-6 Astra that I had checked first. In the sum over the modes of a node, the wires between groups 1 and 3 and between groups 2 and 4 cross when groups 1, 2 and 3, 4 are taken as pairs. Written bit by bit, the two wires are a swap, and the swap splits into a few integer pieces; each piece passes through at most about √12 ≈ 3.46 channels per bit pair instead of 4, and tensor products of 4 × 4 integer matrices move the two pair tables onto those channels, without division and at a small polynomial cost. In the cost model the crossing index then counts as 3.26 units instead of 4, and the mode fraction at seven groups drops from 0.570 to 0.554. Before the loop ran, I checked the algebra with my own programs (both factorisations, their ranks, the fast transforms against dense ones, 216 exact contractions on six groups and more than 600 random nodes of the toy algorithm, where every piece agrees between the two pairings and with a direct evaluation). The referee (version 6) checks the transforms, every piece of 15 hidden nodes and end-to-end counts. Qwen built it in three tasks: the transforms (T23), the crossing route of the mode sum (T24) and the flag in the parameter search (T25). T24 needed two attempts. In the first, Qwen built the table keys by joining two lists instead of sorting them, so terms went missing whenever a mode with a larger number sat next to a crossing mode; three of the fifteen hidden nodes caught it, and a corrected copy confirmed the cause. The second attempt was right from 12:39 on, but Qwen kept adding tests of its own until the 55-minute limit stopped it. T25 then switched the flag on, and the break-even against 2n/3 fell from 663 to 646, exactly as my what-if run had predicted (2,600 to 2,531 against 1.1199ⁿ, 508 to 497 against 1.307ⁿ, 190 to 186 against 1.637ⁿ and 114 to 112 against 2ⁿ).

After round 10 the owner of this page asked whether the much smaller sizes allow a more exact check, so I checked the cost model itself rather than only its outputs. Its three counting formulas are guaranteed upper bounds at every size, not asymptotic estimates: the number of mode tuples follows from a Chernoff-type bound, and the shell products with Strassen and the crossing index from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Exact integer counts with weight budgets up to 2,000 stay below every formula (the tuple counts reach 95% of their bound in bits, the shell products 87 to 90%, closer as the budget grows). A scan of the parameter search from log₂ n = 64 to 3,200 and on to 10⁹ shows the exponent falling steadily and reproduces the five cells exactly, each a size from which the route stays ahead. The factors the model drops are polynomial and cannot move a cell, because n is at least 2⁶⁴ there. What a computation cannot settle are the proofs of the steps, which remain sketches, and one constant: at 646 (round 10) the size is set by the root’s label balance, which asks for about 255.6 wires per label type and has 256.5. That requirement comes from a deliberately generous balancing argument (a simpler bound for a single type would ask for about 14 bits less), so a sharper proof could lower the cell, but by about 1% at most.

Round 11 (2 November, 17:39 to 18:19) built a second proposal of GPT-6 Astra. In the mode sum of a node, fix the exact weight of every group pair (a shell profile); every mode tuple lies in exactly one profile, so the profiles can be summed one by one, each in its own order of the groups. One fixed order has to pay for its worst profile; choosing for each profile the cheapest order of a small menu pays only the minimum, and the minimum is at most the average of the orders’ costs. With the existing bound on the mode tuples this gives an exact certificate: at seven groups the mode fraction drops from 0.554 (one crossing) to 0.474 with Astra’s two orders and to 0.426 with the four orders I found by search; a uniform profile costs the same in every order, so no menu of such orders can go below 0.404. Before the loop ran, I reproduced Astra’s four certificates exactly with my own program and checked the method on more than 750 random nodes of the toy algorithm: every profile value equals a direct sum and is the same in every order. The referee (version 7) checks the order rule, every profile and every matrix product of 14 hidden nodes, the same again with an order chooser of its own, and end-to-end counts. Qwen built it in two tasks: the profile sum (T26) and the flag in the parameter search (T27). Qwen passed both on the first attempt, T26 in 24 minutes and T27 in 90 seconds, and the loop’s result equals my reference solution in every cell.

Hostile review (2 November, evening). The owner of this page sent the brief, the ledger, the frozen cost model, the round-11 certificates and this page to GPT-6 Astra with the request to refute them. Astra reproduced the five certificates and all thirteen profile constants and found no counterexample to the algebra of the profile orders, the binary crossing, the shell products or the guarded deformation (three of them her own proposals, which she was asked to review as if someone else had made them). She found three real problems. First, the cells are not minimal: the unchanged frozen model accepts 100, 157, 532, 411 and 2,033 with a finer choice of δ, Lc and the last level; I reproduced all five exactly, and they are now in the tables. Second, the wire bound for formulas with more clauses omits small connected components; those entries were withdrawn and are recomputed in round 12. Third, several statements needed sharper wording: the formulas have clauses of width at most three, the counting competitor is 2n/3 + o(n), the Appendix D steps must meet their own interface conditions rather than the paper’s Definition 3.1 and Lemmas 4.5 and 4.7, and the dropped costs need an explicit account (see the limits below). She ranks as the weakest links the root extension of the balancing argument, the accounting of memo lookups and tensor-entry evaluation, and the weighted balance behind the leaf step; none is a false identity. With the binary crossing of round 10 switched off every round-11 exponent stays the same, so the cells no longer depend on it. She also proposed a simpler, derandomisable proof of the root label balance that balances each matching of an edge colouring separately; it supports the present condition and, used in its place, would give 92, 148, 526, 403 and 2,033. Round 12 (2 November, evening) put the review into the model; it needed no new code, so the loop had no task. The root label balance by matchings became a step of its own (appd-matchbal) in version 8 of the frozen model. A chunk unit has at most 63·Lc wires (it holds at most 1 + 7(Lc − 1) variables, and its clauses and variables reach outside at most 42·Lc and 21·Lc times), so the wires split into at most 126·Lc − 1 matchings; inside one matching the labels of different wires are independent, each matching is balanced separately with a share of the tolerance, and a union bound over the matchings and label pairs gives a condition that is linear in Lc where the old one was quadratic: 9 to 13 bits less. I checked the degree count against the definition of the units, the tail bound and her five witnesses with my own copy of the model. Three parameter searches then ran on version 8: mine inside the referee’s protocol (the round-11 search with a local refinement, under 3 s per call; the frozen referee scored it at 93, 147, 525, 404 and 2,033), a head differential evolution in two passes, and the review’s own. Break-even sizes

Each cell is a formula size, as log₂ n, from which the cost 2e·n stays below the competitor’s: a feasible threshold of the cost model found by a parameter search, not proved minimal (an outside review found slightly smaller ones for the Appendix D route, now in the table). Smaller is better. The fair columns are the two for formulas with at most three occurrences per variable, the class modelled here; the other two are records for all formulas. The yes/no row answers only whether a formula is satisfiable, with one-sided random error like PPSZ itself, so it has no entries for the two counting columns.

The 1.637ⁿ and 1.307ⁿ records hold for all formulas, but this model covers only the algorithm’s best case, formulas in which every variable occurs at most three times. For exactly those formulas a faster algorithm is known: satisfiability in 1.1199ⁿ (Jain, Neoh and Stephan, 2024). Against that record, the fair comparison for this class, the break-even of the yes/no version after the loop is log₂ n ≈ 196,722 (412,291 after the second round). The Appendix D route would reach 2,032 while counting exactly (code verified by the loop’s referee, cost bounds with proof sketches; see its section). Counting has a faster known method for this class too: the clause–variable graph has maximum degree 3, so its pathwidth is at most about n/3 (Fomin and Høie, 2006), and dynamic programming over a path decomposition counts the solutions in about 2n/3 ≈ 1.26ⁿ. The loop’s counting version beats that only from 43,518; the Appendix D route from 524. The Fable review pointed this out; the loop itself had optimised against 1.307ⁿ.

What a practical break-even would mean. The cells compare worst-case upper bounds; they do not say how long a program runs on a given formula, and practical solvers are judged on real instances. As a scale only: at a break-even the two bounds are equal, against 2n/3 about 2n/3 steps, which is 267 at n = 200, within reach of today’s largest computers, and 2100 at n = 300, far beyond them. A break-even of practical use for worst-case bounds would therefore lie at a few hundred variables, log₂ n ≈ 8. Even against brute force the Appendix D route needs log₂ n = 92 after round 12, about 1028 variables, more than all the world’s storage holds (roughly 1024 bits). In the present design every cell is set by a size condition, in four of the five round-12 dictionaries a label balance condition: the wires are spread over r2u label types, and the balancing argument asks for a margin of wires per type. Against 2n/3 the top level has r = 8 groups and u = 79, so 8158 = 2474 types; the balance by matchings asks for 49.8 more bits, so n must exceed 2523.8, and the threshold is 524. Against 2ⁿ, 722 ≈ 261.8 types and 30.2 bits give 92. The u are this large because a level’s cost falls only like 1/√u. A perfect balancing argument would remove at most the last 50 of the 524 bits. The 474 bits of label types belong to the present labeling design and are not a proven lower bound; GPT-6 Astra asks whether the u coordinate has to carry every label pair. A rough estimate with round 12’s exponent parameters fixed: half the labels would give about 355 against 2n/3 and 61 against 2ⁿ; with no labels at all the padding of the packets would still hold the break-even against 2n/3 at about 225 (n ≈ 1068). No such construction exists yet. Within the present design, further steps like round 11’s measure how far from practice the route is; they do not bring it near.

Appendix D: the tensor-contraction route (cost model and exact checks)

Appendix D proves Theorem 1.1 a different way. The formula becomes a network of one-bit wires, and a recursion over the wires replaces the single global Taylor expansion of Sections 2–4. Its cost exponent shrinks like 1/√(log n), without the extra log log n factor. I modelled its cost on the same class of formulas (every variable in at most three clauses): first with the paper’s constants (r = 64 groups, ε = 2−17, every edge subdivided, h = ⌈3M/u⌉, precision p + 8hDt + 8M(a + 1), 6M/Dt modes of each weight, children with at most 6M/r wires), then with tighter accounting of the same kind as the first audit, then with new steps: four of mine and two suggested in review. In round 5 of the loop (1 November) the code for these steps went through a frozen referee. Each cell is a log₂ n from which the cost stays below the competitor’s, found by a parameter search and not proved minimal.

The new steps have proofs or proof sketches in the loop’s claims ledger. A small exact implementation of Appendix D (packets, the Cauchy deformation, tapered Hermite recovery with 2-adic precision, modes, child networks) checks them on toy formulas. Plain and chunk networks contract to the right count (120 formulas). Recovery from Taylor data cut to the precision of Lemma D.5 returns the right value in 60 cases, and fails in 53 of them with three bits less. The mode expansion computed per tuple as in the paper, with each child once, and group by group agrees with a direct evaluation in 153 node checks (up to 23 modes and 873 tuples). A sub-contraction summed over its chunks’ variables equals the same value in 298 further checks; the toys already pack each pattern to its own granularity. Run end to end at the smallest packet size, the whole algorithm returns the exact count for 17 formulas. With constant labels on the headroom bits it still returns the exact count (7 formulas with four slots per packet and one evaluated level), and the packing needs half the packets (579 instead of 1,141 over 197 formulas). The toys do not test the asymptotic bounds or the label balancing, and they sum the children directly, which is also the model’s best choice. In round 5 the loop’s local model wrote the code for the four code-dependent steps from their descriptions. The frozen referee compared it with my implementation on hidden formulas: 21 node values for each of the three ways of summing the modes, 91 sub-contractions summed over chunk variables and 10 exact end-to-end counts, all equal. Only then did those steps enter the score.

For comparison, Sections 2–4 with the same tighter accounting start at 2.22 million (1.307ⁿ) and 27.7 million (1.1199ⁿ). After four loop rounds they stand at 24,174 and 513,356 for counting, and 10,086 and 196,722 for the yes/no version. Appendix D counts exactly and deterministically. With plain re-accounting it starts about six times lower than Sections 2–4 at the same stage (359,349 against 2.22 million). After round 12 it beats every loop result in the three hard columns: 524 against 2n/3, the fair counting competitor, about 83 times below the loop’s counting version (43,518); 403 against 1.307ⁿ, about 60 times below the loop’s counting result and 25 times below its yes/no result; and 2,032 against the fair 1.1199ⁿ competitor, 253 times below the counting result and 97 times below the yes/no result. Against 1.637ⁿ it needs 147 with the full method; the loop’s counting version reaches 61 there only through its contraction step on its own (marked † in the loop table). The largest single change since the first pass is the sub-contraction step. The paper charges every mode tuple with r fresh sub-contractions, but a sub-contraction depends only on the modes that touch its group, so each distinct one is computed once and a recursion level costs the larger of two terms instead of their sum. That step needs exponential memory, as the loop’s contraction steps already do. A second outside review (Fable), which saw only the first-pass model, independently found the same two facts: every tile carries modes, and a sub-contraction depends only on the modes that touch its group. An outside review (Gemini) checked my three steps and the arithmetic of the model and found no error, and it now agrees that the tile halving from its first review does not hold. It also proposed a second recursion level, which it expected to bring the 1.1199ⁿ column to about 20,000; in the model the gain is smaller (28,442 instead of 30,115), because a deeper level inherits its parent’s precision and high-degree packet vertices, and both cost label space. Once the headroom bits carry no labels, the second level pays in every hard column. After round 5 I looked for further steps and found none with a sound and checkable gain. An outside review (GPT-6 Astra, asked by the owner of this page) then found two that I had missed, both in how each crossing entry is split into modes. The paper writes 1/(A − cj) as a power series in j; Newton’s forward differences write it exactly in binomial coefficients C(j, b), and every term then carries v2(b!) ≈ b extra factors of 2. Factoring the whole crossing block of a packet half as a Cauchy matrix, L·D·U, doubles that again: the pivots lose 2b + 2 v2(b!) factors of 2. Each doubling of the weight growth halves the number of modes per unit of weight and divides the exponent of the mode arithmetic by √2, which halves the break-even size. I proved both steps (closed forms for the Cauchy factors, valuations coefficientwise in z), checked them in exact arithmetic and put them through the loop as round 6. The review also pointed out that no gate had executed the second recursion level that the best scores use; that audit is described in the loop section. The claims ledger lists the remaining ideas examined and why each was set aside. Three more outside reviews (GPT-6 Astra, Gemini and Fable, again asked by the owner of this page) followed in round 7. Astra’s proposal to do each group-by-group step as fast matrix multiplication went through the loop; its finite-size bound for the LDU weights is now a proof in the ledger, so the cost model’s mode count is an upper bound at every size, not only an asymptotic estimate. The other proposals were checked and set aside, with the reasons in the ledger. Asked about Fable’s open question, GPT-6 Astra then found a different deformation in round 8: empty guard slots at both ends of every packet let a Lagrange interpolation polynomial of half the degree do the work of the paper’s Cauchy deformation, with the same mode weights and half the 2-adic precision. It went through the loop as well. In round 9 Astra proposed two more steps, a Chinese-remainder reset of the children’s precision and a label balance that charges parallel wires instead of the maximum degree; both hold, but neither changes a break-even here, because every binding constraint now sits at the root. In round 10 Astra turned to the crossing idea of Anthropic’s paper and found a version that is cheap to carry out: two wires that cross between four groups are split into a few pieces, each of which passes through fewer channels than the two wires together, and integer transforms move the tables onto those channels without any division. I checked it independently and the loop built it; it lowers every break-even by about 2 to 3 percent. In round 11 Astra found that one fixed order of the groups, however good, pays for the worst weight profile; choosing the cheapest of two orders separately for each profile is cheaper, with a short exact certificate. I checked it, extended it to menus of three and four orders, and the loop built it: about 17 percent off the break-even against 2n/3.

Formulas with more clauses (repaired in round 12). Every row above is for formulas in which each variable occurs at most three times. A hostile review (GPT-6 Astra) found that the wire bound used here omits small connected components: one clause on three variables has no crossing wire, while 2m − n + n/Lc = −1/4 at Lc = 4. Round 12 solves small components directly; when every variable occurs in some clause, a connected component with c clauses has at most 2c + 1 variables, so the large components carry at most 2m − 2n/3 + n/Lc wires. The root balance by matchings uses the exact unit degree 3B(2B + 1)·Lc for an occurrence cap B in place of the old scaling argument. For a 3-CNF with m clauses and no limit on occurrences, the chunk networks of the large components therefore have at most 2m − 2n/3 + n/Lc wires instead of about n (each clause contributes two wires, each variable inside a chunk saves one, and the small components hand back at most a third of a wire per variable), so the break-even grows roughly with the square of the clause density. At m = 4.26n, the density at which random formulas are hardest, the repaired model with round 12’s steps gives 6,594 against 1.637ⁿ, 22,223 against 1.307ⁿ and 3,357 against 2ⁿ (the withdrawn entries were 6,063, 20,415 and 3,089). At m = 5n it gives 9,293, 31,365 and 4,722. For comparison, Anthropic’s own paper on the question states that its displayed bound needs n above 1010,000 to improve on exhaustive search at m = 5n: a threshold for that bound, not a certified crossover, and a later preprint (Majdoddin, 30 October) lowers the crossover points of that algorithm. The repaired entry here is about 101,421 against 2ⁿ; the two compare different algorithms under different analyses. Variables with more than 1,024 occurrences are first split into chains of copies, which adds no wires (each equality is one arity-two constraint); the root balance then pays for the larger unit degree, about 17 bits more than at three occurrences. The 2n/3 and 1.1199ⁿ competitors exist only for three occurrences. Fable computed this entry independently for round 6’s steps (55,420 and 16,384; this page’s model gives 55,416 and 16,381 there).

Limits of this audit

  • Bounded-occurrence 3-CNF (clauses of width at most three, each variable in at most three clauses) is the algorithm’s best case. With exactly three distinct variables per clause and at most three occurrences every formula is satisfiable (Hall’s theorem), so the yes/no comparison needs width at most three. General formulas go through sparsification and parity tests (Sections 5–6), whose product degree is about 8k·n instead of 3n, so their thresholds are higher still. The Appendix D section gives entries for general formulas at clause densities 4.26 and 5, recomputed in round 12 after a correction found in review: 22,223 and 31,365 against 1.307ⁿ.
  • The chart, the break-even tables and the loop model the interpolation algorithm of Sections 2–6. The tensor-contraction algorithm of Appendix D has its own section and one row in the break-even table: a cost model, head proofs or proof sketches for thirteen new steps, toy-scale exact checks (including two recursion levels executed end to end) and, in rounds 5 to 11, code verified by the loop’s referee (round 12 changed only the model and the parameter search) on small formulas; no large run.
  • Only exponents are compared. Two kinds of cost are dropped: polynomial factors, and the evaluation of packet and chunk tensor entries (exp(O(Q log s)) per packet entry with Q = 2a, and 2O(Lc) per chunk entry). The review’s scale for the second kind is 2a(log₂ n + a) bits; at every Appendix D cell the margin n·(competitor’s exponent − e) is larger by a factor of at least 246, so neither can plausibly move a cell, but a full finite-runtime comparison has to count them explicitly.
  • 1.637ⁿ (#3-SAT) and 1.307ⁿ (3-SAT, decision only) are the best bounds as cited by the paper; both are for general formulas. For the class modelled here (every variable in at most three clauses) satisfiability is known to take 1.1199ⁿ (arXiv 2411.07389), the fair yes/no competitor, and counting takes 2n/3 + o(n) by dynamic programming over a path decomposition (Fomin and Høie, 2006, with the usual reduction to the degree-three vertices), the fair counting competitor; comparing with 2n/3 without the o(n) is conservative here. A better counting bound for this class may exist; I did not find one.
  • The paper’s theorem is not yet independently confirmed. As of 31 October 2026 the review led by Ryan Williams with Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi had not reported, and OpenAI says the result was checked in Lean without saying which statement the formal proof covers. Every number on this page assumes that the paper’s construction is correct; the code here checks its identities on small instances, not the theorem.
  • The contraction steps of the loop (T12, T14) and the Appendix D steps trade the paper’s polynomial memory for exponential memory, tables of about 20.36n entries at the break-even. PPSZ and the 1.1199ⁿ algorithm run in polynomial memory; the 2n/3 counting method needs exponential memory as well.
  • The code tests the construction on small instances. It checks the mechanics; it does not prove the theorem.
  • The loop’s extensions (two-stage balancing, the δ tolerance, direct reads, grouping per U, clause units, chunk units, the yes/no count modulo a prime, per-point measurements, equal-pair windows, the small prime with interpolation points in GF(p²), the contraction of the group sums and the edge bits) rest on proof sketches written during the loop. The referee’s exact-count tests exercise the constructions and identities on small formulas, but not the asymptotic bounds.
